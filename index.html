VectorLLM

Language-Controllable Autonomous Driving via Vectorized Scene Grounding

VectorLLM turns road geometry and agent motion into discrete tokens an LLM can read alongside language. The result is one model that answers questions about a scene and adapts its planned motion when the scene or instruction changes.

Abstract

Ground driving language in precise scene structure

Language is useful for interacting with a vehicle only when the model can connect instructions to the road and traffic around it—and change its behavior when either changes. Existing driving LLMs often pass scenes through images, continuous features, or text descriptions. VectorLLM instead discretizes map geometry and agent motion into vector tokens and adds them to a pretrained decoder-only LLM’s vocabulary. Across WOMD and nuScenes, it improves driving question answering and responds to human-annotated counterfactual scene edits and instructions, without training on those counterfactual evaluations. Representation ablations show that keeping scene vectors as discrete LLM tokens is important to these gains.

The problem

Driving language needs a precise scene representation

An instruction such as “yield to the car ahead” depends on who is ahead, how fast they are moving, and where the road goes. Compressing that structure into visual features or prose can make precise geometry and interactions harder for an LLM to use. VectorLLM represents those scene elements directly in the same discrete sequence as language.

1. Ego-centric vectorization

Coordinates are translated and rotated into the ego vehicle’s local frame. Static map polylines and dynamic agent histories are both expressed as ordered two-dimensional displacement vectors. Local map context is filtered around the ego trajectory, and curvature-adaptive resampling uses shorter segments in curved regions and longer segments on near-straight roads.

2. Discrete scene vocabulary

A k-disks procedure builds a 1,024-token vocabulary of displacement vectors from WOMD. Beam-search assignment limits reconstruction drift along long polylines; road and trajectory polylines are reconstructed with error below 0.03 m. Semantic markers distinguish maps, roads, ego motion, and agents, while delimiter tokens mark sequence boundaries.

3. One serialized context

Map blocks, ego history, surrounding-agent histories, and an optional question or instruction are concatenated into one sequence. The original language vocabulary is preserved; vector and special tokens expand both the input embedding matrix and the language-model output head.

4. LLM-centered architecture

A pretrained decoder-only LLM processes the shared sequence. The model adds vector and semantic tokens to its vocabulary and fully fine-tunes the LLM, with no separate scene encoder or feature projector.

5. Grounded chain of thought

Reasoning traces follow a driving-oriented sequence: identify ego motion, select relevant map and nearby-agent evidence, reason about interactions and feasible maneuvers, then support the answer or trajectory. Vector-token references keep the intermediate reasoning tied to the serialized scene.

6. Goal-conditioned trajectory decoding

A goal head predicts sparse future waypoints. Trajectory positions then attend to one another and are decoded in parallel as vector tokens, which are mapped back to displacement vectors and accumulated into metric coordinates.

Training

Learn scene structure first, then solve driving tasks

Stage I · Vector-token pretraining

Standard next-token prediction over serialized driving scenes exposes the pretrained LLM to map and motion structures as ordinary token sequences.

Stage II · Multi-task fine-tuning

Question answering, goal prediction, and trajectory generation are trained together. Language and vector outputs use cross-entropy; the continuous waypoint goal head uses an L2 loss.

Grounded supervision generation

WOMD supplies map elements, agent histories, and future trajectories. A deterministic pipeline extracts motion events, map context, spatial relations, interactions, counts, and goal-oriented cues. Rule-based analysis determines the labels and relevant scene evidence; Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 only paraphrases those facts into natural-language QA pairs and reasoning traces. It is not asked to infer geometry or behavior from raw trajectories.

Reference implementation setting

The main experiments use Qwen3-0.6B, a 1,024-token scene vocabulary, and a 12,288-token training context. Only 0.6% of WOMD training scenes exceed that context limit and are dropped.

Evaluation

Test scene understanding and behavior change

We evaluate language grounding on WOMD-Reasoning and nuScenes-QA. To test controllability, we introduce two human-annotated counterfactual benchmarks: 300 WOMD validation scenes with edits to lane geometry, agents, or ego history, and 200 nuScenes test scenes with alternative feasible driving instructions. These counterfactual sets are held out; VectorLLM receives no task-specific counterfactual training.

Question answering

Answer scene-dependent questions about map geometry, motion, and agent interactions.

Scene edits

Change the map, nearby agents, or ego history and measure whether the planned response follows.

Language steering

Keep the scene fixed, change the instruction, and test whether generated trajectories follow it.

Controllability is measured as best-of-six target reachability: a generated trajectory succeeds when it passes within 1.0 m of the human-annotated target waypoint at its specified horizon. VectorLLM+MapTR replaces annotated HD maps with map vectors predicted from multiview images by a pretrained MapTR model.

Results

Discrete scene tokens improve grounding and control

VectorLLM outperforms the evaluated baselines on both question-answering benchmarks and on held-out counterfactual scene edits and language steering. With MapTR-predicted maps, performance remains competitive, showing the approach can use perception-derived vectors.

What drives the results

Discrete tokens and useful reasoning both matter

Two controlled comparisons isolate the representation and the inference-time reasoning. Continuous scene embeddings underperform when the scene input is held constant, and incorrect or missing reasoning lowers both QA and instruction-steering scores.

Latent-space alignment

t-SNE visualizations place maneuver language near related trajectory tokens and map-context language near relevant map tokens. This is qualitative evidence that the shared context learns semantically meaningful vector-language relationships.

Attention-based grounding

Selected language tokens attend to scene regions relevant to the question or instruction, and the attended regions shift under counterfactual commands. The paper treats these maps as qualitative grounding evidence, not definitive causal attribution.

Unseen scene captioning

Without captioning supervision, the model can produce descriptions combining ego motion, road context, and nearby-agent interactions. This is presented as qualitative generalization rather than a benchmarked captioning result.

What attention reveals

Attention shifts toward relevant road geometry and agents as the question, instruction, or scene changes. The paper presents these visualizations as qualitative evidence of grounding, not causal proof.

Limitations

What this evaluation does and does not establish

  • Structured inputs are assumed. The main model consumes vectorized HD-map elements; MapTR-predicted maps test a perception-based input, but the paper does not jointly train raw perception and language control.
  • Open-loop reachability is not closed-loop safety. The counterfactual score checks whether one of six predicted paths reaches an annotated waypoint; it does not establish safe behavior during closed-loop driving.
  • The counterfactual sets are limited in scope. They contain 300 edited WOMD scenes and 200 nuScenes instruction scenes, annotated by 15 people.
  • Some evidence is qualitative. Attention maps, latent-space visualizations, and unseen-scene captions illustrate behavior but are not independently scored benchmarks.
